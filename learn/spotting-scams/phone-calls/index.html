Scam phone calls

In this lesson

  • The scam calls that cost people the most
  • What real banks and police will never ask you to do
  • The safe way to check a call, using 159

Phone scams cost people the most money, and the people most often targeted are over 70. The caller sounds professional and friendly, and the number on your screen can be faked to look like your bank's or the police's.

The calls to watch for

  • "Your bank's fraud team": they say your account is at risk and ask you to move your money to a "safe account". There is no such thing. This is always a scam.
  • "The police": they say they're investigating your bank and need you to withdraw cash, buy gold, or hand your cards to a courier. Real police never do this.
  • Computer "support": someone from "Microsoft", "BT" or your broadband says your computer has a problem and asks you to install an app so they can fix it. That app lets them into your computer.
  • "Survey" calls: questions about your health, money or home. They can record your voice and use a computer copy of it to set up payments.
  • "Government schemes": cold callers offering free insulation, boilers or solar panels on a scheme that has closed or never existed.

The safe way to check a call

Hang up. You can always call back. Then call 159, which puts you through to your own bank's real number, or use the number on the back of your card.

If you can, use a different phone. On a landline, scammers can keep the line open after you hang up, so you'd still be talking to them.

To cut down scam calls, most phones can send calls from numbers that aren't in your contacts straight to voicemail. BT landline customers can dial 1572 to block the last number that called.

Try it yourself: what would you do?

Pick what you'd do in each situation.

Call 1 of 4

"Hello, this is Sam from your bank's fraud team. Someone is trying to take money from your account right now. To keep it safe, we need you to move it to a new safe account we've set up for you."

A "safe account" always means a scam. Asking them to prove it doesn't help, because scammers often already know your name, address and bank details.

Call 2 of 4

"This is Detective Constable Harris. We think staff at your bank are involved in fraud. We need your help: please withdraw £3,000 and a courier will collect it as evidence. Don't tell the bank why."

Police will never ask you to withdraw money, buy gold or hand anything to a courier, and never ask you to keep it from your bank. If a courier is on the way, call 999.

Call 3 of 4

"I'm calling from your broadband provider. Your computer is sending out viruses. I need you to go to your computer and install an app called AnyDesk so I can fix it for you."

No real company calls out of the blue about viruses. Apps like that let the caller control your computer and get into your online banking. If you're worried about your computer, take it to someone you trust.

Call 4 of 4

"Hi, we're doing a quick lifestyle survey for a £50 voucher. Can you confirm you're the homeowner? Just say yes. And do you have any health conditions?"

These calls collect your details and record your voice, which can be copied and used to agree to payments. You don't have to be polite to a cold caller: just hang up. Keep an eye on your bank statement afterwards.

Remember

  • Your bank or the police will never ask you to move money, withdraw cash or hand cards to a courier.
  • If a call worries you, hang up. You can always call back on a number you trust.
  • Call 159 to reach your own bank safely. Use a different phone if you can.
  • Never let a caller onto your computer, and never install an app because a caller asks.
  • Hang up on "survey" calls asking about your health or money. Your voice can be recorded and copied.
