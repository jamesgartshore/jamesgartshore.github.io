Fake websites, adverts and pop-ups

In this lesson

  • How to read a web address to see who really owns a site
  • Why a padlock doesn't mean a site is safe
  • How to spot fake shops, celebrity adverts and scary pop-ups

Scam websites can look exactly like the real thing. The good news is that one small part of every website address tells you who really owns it, and it can't be faked.

How to read a web address

Look at the part just before the first single "/". That's the site's real name. Everything after it can say anything.

https://www.gov.uk/renew-adult-passport GOV.UK, the real government site

https://www.passport-renewal-uk.com/gov.uk/apply Not GOV.UK, even though it says "gov.uk" later

https://royalmail.parcel-redelivery.co/track Owned by "parcel-redelivery.co", not Royal Mail

Official UK government websites end in gov.uk. Copycat sites for passports, driving licences and the GHIC health card look official but charge extra fees. To be safe, type the address yourself instead of using a search result or advert.

The padlock doesn't mean it's safe

The little padlock next to a web address only means your connection is private. Scam websites have padlocks too. It tells you nothing about whether the people running the site are honest.

Fake adverts and shops

  • Celebrity adverts: Martin Lewis and other famous names don't advertise investments. Those adverts and videos are fakes, sometimes made by computer.
  • Amazing bargains: huge discounts on new websites, often seen on Facebook, are a common way to steal card details. Search the shop's name with the word "reviews" first.
  • How you pay matters: pay by credit card or PayPal, which can protect you. If a shop only accepts bank transfer, walk away.

Scary pop-ups

A message saying "Your phone has a virus!" or "Call Microsoft now", sometimes with sirens or flashing, is just an advert on a web page. It can't see your phone or computer. Close the tab or app, and never call the number on it.

Try it yourself: which is the real website?

Use the trick above: look just before the first single "/".

Question 1 of 3: renewing your passport

Only gov.uk is the government. "gov-uk-passports.co.uk" is owned by whoever bought that name, and copycat sites like these charge extra.

Question 2 of 3: tracking a parcel

The real one is royalmail.com. In "royalmail.track-redelivery.com" the site is really "track-redelivery.com", and "Royal Mail" is just a word added to the front.

Question 3 of 3: checking your State Pension

Only gov.uk is real. "gov.uk.pension-checker.com" is a sneaky one: the real name is "pension-checker.com", right before the first "/".

Remember

  • Read the part of a web address just before the first single "/". That's who really owns the site.
  • Official UK government websites end in gov.uk. Lookalike sites often charge extra fees.
  • A padlock only means the connection is private. It doesn't mean the site is honest.
  • Famous people don't advertise investments. Check shops' reviews and pay by credit card, not bank transfer.
  • A scary "virus" pop-up is just an advert. Close it, and never call the number on it.
